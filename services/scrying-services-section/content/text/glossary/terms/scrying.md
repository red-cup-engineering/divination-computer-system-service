**Scrying** is a divinatory practice in which a practitioner gazes into a reflective, translucent, or otherwise visually ambiguous surface — a [scrying medium](scrying-medium.md) — to receive images, impressions, or insights. Common media include crystal balls, dark mirrors, bowls of water, fire, and smoke.

The mechanism, from a naturalistic perspective, is pareidolia and hypnagogic imagery: the visual ambiguity of the medium provides a substrate onto which the practitioner's subconscious projects patterns. From a spiritual perspective, the medium serves as a threshold between ordinary perception and a mode of seeing that is not constrained by physical stimulus.

Scrying is among the oldest documented divinatory practices, appearing across cultures with no obvious contact — in ancient Egyptian water-gazing, Aztec obsidian mirrors, Celtic fire-watching, and Elizabethan-era crystal work (most famously associated with John Dee and Edward Kelley). The universality of the practice suggests that the underlying cognitive phenomenon — finding meaning in visual noise — is deeply human rather than culturally specific.

As a form of [sign](../../../../../../science/domains/linguistics/domains/semiotics/terms/sign.md) interpretation, scrying is notable for the extreme openness of its signifying material. Where tarot provides a fixed set of images with established interpretive traditions, scrying provides raw visual ambiguity. This makes it simultaneously more personal and harder to teach: there is no deck to memorize, no [spread](../../tarot/terms/spread.md) to structure the reading — only the practitioner's capacity to hold attention and recognize meaning when it surfaces.
